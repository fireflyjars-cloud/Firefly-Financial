The Banking Migration Map

Taking the Jarring Framework off paper and into real bank accounts.

🔥

Before you start. This guide deliberately doesn't tell you which bank to use. It grew out of my own household's move, but the framework works at any bank that lets you hold several named sub-accounts — and the one you already have may do it. None of this is financial advice, and no bank pays me anything for it. Your situation, income, and existing accounts are yours to weigh: check current terms yourself, and for anything involving taxes or closing accounts, talk to a professional who knows your circumstances.

If You're Self-Employed, Read This First

Most banks reserve their best savings rate for customers with a payroll direct deposit — which self-employed people don't have. Before you pick a bank, ask them directly: “I have no payroll direct deposit. What else qualifies me for your highest rate?” Many banks accept a minimum amount transferred in each period instead, and some offer a paid membership that unlocks the rate regardless. Both are workable; you just have to know to ask.

Never take a rate from a page like this one. Savings rates and qualifying rules change constantly, including at whichever bank you choose. Get the current number from the bank itself before you open anything, and ask what happens to your rate in a month where you fall short.

📋 WHAT TO LOOK FOR IN A BANK

This system needs one thing your bank may or may not offer: the ability to hold several named sub-accounts inside one savings account. Different banks call them vaults, buckets, spaces, envelopes, or simply multiple savings accounts — the name doesn't matter, the separation does. Before you move anything, check that yours can do it. Many banks already can, and you may not need to switch at all.

Worth confirming while you're asking: no monthly maintenance fee, no minimum balance, a debit card that pulls from checking rather than savings, and how many sub-accounts you're allowed. Five is the minimum this framework needs. More lets you break each jar into specific goals.

Step-by-Step Migration Guide

The exact order of operations — do these in sequence, not all at once.

⏱️

Timeline: Give This 45–60 Days

This is not a weekend project. Rushing a banking move is how autopays break and payments get missed — and a missed payment can cost you far more than the interest you were chasing. Work through the phases in order, and keep your existing account open and funded the entire time.

1

PHASE 1 — Know Your Starting Number

Before you open anything, settle the one figure this whole system runs on: how much money arrives, and when.

  • Pick your Pour Day — one day each week, every week. Consistency matters more than which day
  • Work out your typical weekly income. If it varies, use a conservative recent average rather than your best week
  • Make a list of every bill on autopay and which account it currently pulls from. You'll need this list in Phase 2
🏢 If you run a business: keep business and personal money in separate accounts first, and pay yourself a set weekly amount from the business account. Pouring jars directly from business income means spending money you owe in taxes. If yours are currently mixed together, sort that out before anything else — and talk to a bookkeeper or accountant about the right structure for your situation.
2

Set Up an Account That Can Hold Sub-Accounts

  • Check your current bank first. If it already supports named sub-accounts, buckets, or vaults, you may not need to move at all — skip to Step 3
  • If it doesn't, compare a few options on: number of sub-accounts allowed, monthly fees, minimum balance, and how you'd qualify for their best savings rate
  • Open the new account and fund it with a small transfer to activate it
  • Wait for the debit card to arrive before you move any bills — usually 7–10 business days
  • Sharing finances with someone? Decide deliberately between a joint account and separate accounts. Both work with this framework
📝 Leave your existing account open. You'll need it as a fallback for weeks, and closing it early is the single most common way a banking move goes wrong.
3

Create Your 5 Named Sub-Accounts

However your bank does this — vaults, buckets, spaces, or separate savings accounts — create five and name them exactly:

  • 🌱 Genesis Jar™ — Investing & Multiplication
  • 🚪 Exodus Jar™ — Playing & Joy
  • 📜 Leviticus Jar™ — Learning & Self-Development
  • 🔢 Numbers Jar™ — Savings & Discipline
  • 📣 Deuteronomy Jar™ — Giving & Generosity
📌 The exact names matter more than you'd think. A sub-account called "Savings" gets raided. One called Deuteronomy Jar™ — Giving & Generosity does not. The name is the boundary.
4

Move Your Bills Over — One at a Time

This is the step people rush and regret. Spread it across 2–3 weeks:

  • Update one biller at a time, either on their website or by phone. Get your new account and routing numbers from your bank's app or a statement — never from a page or email that "reminds" you what they are
  • Keep a simple list: bill name → old account → moved on → first successful payment confirmed
  • Confirm each one actually processed before you move the next. "I updated it" is not the same as "it went through"
  • Keep enough money in your old account to cover anything you've missed
🗂️ Easy ones to forget: insurance, annual subscriptions, the gym, storage units, anything that bills quarterly, and any card saved with an online retailer.
5

Run Your First Weekly Pour

Once the account is open and the five sub-accounts exist, do your first pour by hand:

  • Make sure this week's income has landed in your checking account
  • Use the Weekly Calculator tab to work out each amount
  • Transfer each amount into its named sub-account, one at a time
  • Watch them fill. This is the whole system in one motion
Do it manually for the first few weeks so you can feel the rhythm. Once it's routine, automate it if your bank allows scheduled transfers.
6

Confirm You Actually Qualify for the Rate You Expected

Advertised savings rates almost always come with conditions. Get yours confirmed in writing rather than assuming:

  • Ask your bank exactly what qualifies you for the higher rate, and what the rate falls to if you don't qualify in a given period
  • If you're paid irregularly, ask specifically: "Do incoming transfers from my own other account count as qualifying deposits?" The answer varies by bank
  • Ask whether the rate applies to money sitting in sub-accounts, or only the main balance
  • If they offer a paid membership that unlocks the rate, do the arithmetic before subscribing — a monthly fee can easily cost more than the extra interest on a small balance
🧮 Worth a sanity check: on a $2,000 balance, the difference between 1% and 4% is about $60 a year. Real, but not worth a $10/month membership to capture. The bigger your balance, the more the rate matters.
7

PHASE 3 — Decide What Your Old Account Is For

Notice this step is not called "close your old account." Once a full billing cycle has passed with every payment clearing successfully, you have a choice to make — and closing isn't automatically the right one.

  • Good reasons to keep it open: a relationship that gets you better loan or mortgage rates, a long account history, a local branch you actually use, or simply a fallback if something goes wrong
  • Good reasons to close it: monthly fees you're paying for nothing, or so many accounts that you can't keep track
  • Either way, keep it open with a small balance for at least a month after your last bill moves. Stragglers surface late
  • If you do close one, do it deliberately — move the balance first, get confirmation in writing, and don't leave a card active on a closed account
⚠️ Credit unions in particular often give members meaningfully better rates on car loans and mortgages. If that's true for you, closing the account to chase a savings rate can cost you far more than it earns. Think it through before you act.

Sub-Account Setup Guide

Exact names, purposes, and the rule for each jar — plus how to break a jar into specific goals.

🔧 HOW TO CREATE THEM

Every bank words this differently, but the path is nearly always the same. In your banking app, look for:

  1. Your savings account
  2. An option called Vaults, Buckets, Spaces, Envelopes, or Goals
  3. Something like + Create or + New
  4. Enter the name exactly as listed below — including the jar name, not just "savings"
  5. Set a target amount if it offers one, then save
  6. Repeat for all five jars
Can't find the feature? Ask support: "Can I create multiple named savings sub-accounts inside my account, and how many?" If the answer is no, opening five separate savings accounts works too — it's just more logins.
Sub-Account Name Allocation Rule & Purpose
🌱 Genesis Jar™Investing & Multiplication 10% Locked until your investment goal is reached. This jar feeds your future — stock account, real estate fund, or business investment. Do not touch it for anything else.
🚪 Exodus Jar™Playing & Joy 10% Spent completely every month — no carrying over. This defeats the "I deserve it" impulse spending by giving joy a proper container. Dining out, date night, fun purchases — all come from here.
📜 Leviticus Jar™Learning & Self-Development 10% Books, courses, certifications, seminars. You are your greatest asset — this jar is the one that invests in you rather than in things.
🔢 Numbers Jar™Savings & Discipline 10% Builds your financial cushion and funds named future purchases. Break it into specific goals: Emergency Fund, Vehicle, Home Repair, Vacation. Never touch it for impulse purchases.
📣 Deuteronomy Jar™Giving & Generosity 10% Tithes, offerings, charity, blessing others. Giving blesses the whole system — this jar is not optional. It is the jar that keeps the system moving.

Breaking a Jar Into Specific Goals

If your bank allows more than five sub-accounts, use the extras to name what you're actually saving for.

🌱 INSIDE GENESIS (examples)

Investment Account Funding · House Down Payment · Retirement Seed Fund · Business Reinvestment

🔢 INSIDE NUMBERS (the most common ones)

Emergency Fund (3–6 months of expenses) · Vehicle Replacement · Home Repair Reserve · Tax Reserve · Insurance Deductible · Irregular-Income Buffer

📜 INSIDE LEVITICUS (examples)

Certification Fund · Coaching or Mentorship · Conference Travel · Course Library

💡 IF YOU'RE SELF-EMPLOYED: THE TAX RESERVE

This is the sub-account that saves people the most grief. If you're self-employed, you likely owe quarterly estimated taxes, and that money was never yours to spend. Create a Tax Reserve inside Numbers and move a set percentage of every payment into it the moment it arrives — before it can feel like income.

A common starting point is 25–30% of net profit, but your actual rate depends on your income, filing status, deductions, and state — so confirm your number with a tax professional rather than trusting a rule of thumb. Whatever the figure, having it sitting in a named account earning interest is what turns tax season from a crisis into a transfer.
